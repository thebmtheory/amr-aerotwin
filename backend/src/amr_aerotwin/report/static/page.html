I lined up every driver's best qualifying lap on a shared distance grid and split the gap to the fastest lap into corners and straights. Below is what came out and how sure I am of each part, then the physics and data science behind it and how I handled the data.

    Findings 01 and 02 | Corners and straights

    Loss rates, team by team

    Each team's best lap is lined up with the pole lap, and the time difference is split into corners (braking, apex and exit, either side of each corner) and everything in between. Dividing the time lost in each part by the time the pole lap spends there gives a loss rate. Points below the diagonal lose less per second on the straights than in the corners.

    How I did it

    From raw timing data to this page

    Six steps. The figure under each one comes from this run of the pipeline.

    1. 01

      Collect

      Each driver's fastest qualifying lap with its car data and GPS, and the official times of every qualifying and race lap, from a public archive of F1 live timing.

      Python, requests, pandas

    2. 02

      Check

      Automated checks on every lap catch broken telemetry: frozen or stalled channels, dropouts, GPS that disagrees with the wheel-speed sensor, and clocks that don't match.

      Bounds set from the spread on clean laps

    3. 03

      Align

      Each lap is projected onto the reference lap's line on a shared distance grid. Its GPS and car-data clocks are matched, and it's re-timed to the timing line.

      Projection onto a reference line, signal matching, smoothing splines

    4. 04

      Physics

      Speed, acceleration, curvature and gradient are worked out along every lap. A point-mass lap simulator, driven by the track's curvature, is calibrated to the pole lap and to Aston Martin's best lap at each event.

      Vehicle dynamics, friction ellipse, Bayesian calibration

    5. 05

      Model

      Statistics built for small samples, an emulator and a reduced-order model of the simulator, and machine learning only on what the physics misses.

      Bootstrap, Bayesian changepoints, difference-in-differences, Gaussian processes, POD, gradient boosting, conformal prediction

    6. 06

      Report

      This page and the technical note are written from the results every time the pipeline runs. Tests check the wording against the data, and that the simulator in your browser matches the Python one.

      Plotly, JavaScript, pytest

      No result on this page is typed by hand.

    The physics and data science

    How I tested what I found

    Timing data shows where a car is slow. It can't say why, or how far to trust any single number. So I built a small stack of models around the findings. Each one answers one narrow question, and where I can, I check it on data it wasn't fitted to.

    01 | Lap simulator

    A physics model of the lap

    A quasi-steady point-mass simulator. A few numbers describe the car: grip, downforce, drag, power, and how much of the grip is left for braking and traction. Grip is μ(g + qCLA/m) on a friction ellipse, with a power limit and drag on top. Give it a track's shape from GPS and it returns a speed trace and a lap time.

    05 | Discrepancy model

    Mapping where the physics is wrong

    What the simulator misses isn't random. It comes in runs through whole corners and braking zones. A Gaussian process maps that error along the lap, which shows where a point-mass model falls short and by how much.

    The band includes measurement noise, so it's a predictive band, not only the uncertainty of the curve.

            Limits

            What this can't tell you

            What public data can't tell

            Whether the straight-line loss comes from power, drag or the way the battery is used.

            Absolute grip or downforce. Every physical number here is an effective value for a simplified car.

            What team data would add

            ERS state of charge and deployment would split the straight-line loss into engine output, deployment and drag.

            Ride heights and wind-tunnel maps would turn the grip line into a track-to-tunnel correlation check.

            Tyre temperatures and pressures would replace effective grip with a tyre model.
